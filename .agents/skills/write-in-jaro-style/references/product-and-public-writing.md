# Product and Public Writing

Jaro's public voice is thoughtful without being solemn, conversational without being loose, and opinionated without pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

## Shape

- Open with a concrete observation, honest tension, or crisp claim. Skip the formal introduction.
- Move between the personal and the general: a specific moment earns the broader point.
- Use ordinary analogies to make abstractions visible. Prefer familiar objects and situations to fashionable jargon.
- Build arguments through contrasts: what something appears to be versus what it is, what helps versus what harms, or what changed between then and now.
- Let rhetorical questions carry the reader from one thought to the next, but answer or develop them.
- Break dense ideas into short paragraphs. Mix compact sentences with the occasional longer, accumulating sentence.
- Close with the sharpest implication, a genuine open question, or a callback. Some subjects do not need a neat moral.

## Personality

- Use first and second person naturally.
- Include dry humour, mild self-deprecation, and parenthetical asides when they sharpen the observation.
- Use culturally or geographically specific detail only when supplied by Jaro or supported by the source material.
- Make room for qualification: “I think”, “perhaps”, and explicit limits can make a claim more trustworthy, but do not hedge every sentence.
- Prefer warmth to grandeur. People are people, not examples enlisted to prove a point.

## Intensity

Use a restrained setting by default: a clean opening, one strong analogy at most, light humour, and a concise ending.

Turn up the playfulness for personal essays, informal product copy, or when Jaro explicitly asks. Even then, avoid stacked jokes, exaggerated outrage, and cleverness that obscures the point.

## Things that do not sound like Jaro

- A generic scene invented to create false intimacy.
- Corporate optimism or breathless product language.
- A perfectly balanced essay whose rough human edges have been polished away.
- Constant em dashes, slogan-like fragments, or suspiciously tidy groups of three.
- A conclusion that announces “in conclusion” and repeats the whole argument.
- Confidence unsupported by evidence.
